# Speech Memorization Research Synthesis

> Research synthesis informing `lifecycle-coaching-prd.md`. For a normal professional preparing a 2-min toast over 5 days. Compiled 2026-05-05.

## Executive summary

The research converges on a small set of high-leverage techniques: **active retrieval** (not rereading), **distributed practice** (not cramming), **production** (out-loud, not silent), **meaning-based encoding** (not rote), and **embodied rehearsal** (movement + emotion). The biggest evidence-based wins come from replacing the default behavior — *read the script silently many times in one sitting* — with short, frequent, recall-from-memory sessions.

---

## 1. Foundational techniques for memorizing prose

### 1.1 Whole vs. part learning
**Evidence: moderate, mixed.** Meaning and organization favor whole-method (the parts make sense in relation to each other), but for difficult or long material, part-based learning can outperform — especially for less-practiced learners.

**Application:** A 2-min toast is short enough for whole-method, but Daniel isn't a memory expert, so a **hybrid** — chunk into 4–6 sections, *but* run the whole speech end-to-end at least once per session. Don't drill chunk 1 to perfection before chunk 2 exists in his head.

### 1.2 Progressive part / cumulative part method
**Evidence: well-established in motor learning, plausible for prose.** Practice part 1, then 1+2, then 1+2+3 — chaining. This directly addresses the bridge/transition weakness because transitions get practiced *every* repetition.

**Application:** Strong fit for a short toast. Cumulative ensures Daniel never has a "fresh" transition that's only been practiced once.

### 1.3 Method of loci (memory palace)
**Evidence: strong for ordered lists; weaker for verbatim prose.** Lab novices triple recall on lists; MRI shows hippocampal-parietal restructuring. Greeks invented it for orators. But: shines for *content order*, not word-for-word; high setup cost.

**Application:** Overkill for a 2-min toast for a non-trained user. The underlying principle (anchor each chunk to a vivid image) is extractable; a lightweight "image per chunk" beats a full palace.

### 1.4 Chunking and chunk size
**Evidence: very strong (Miller, Cowan).** Working memory holds ~4 ± 1 *meaningful* chunks. Chunk size is determined by *meaning*, not word count.

**Application:** Break a 2-min toast into 4–6 chunks of 15–30 seconds each (~30–60 words). Align with rhetorical units (setup → story → turn → punchline → toast), not sentence breaks.

### 1.5 First-letter / cue-word mnemonics
**Evidence: moderate, classic.** First-letter mnemonics work primarily by **preserving word order**, not making each word easier to recall. Actors use this as a mid-rehearsal scaffold: drop the script, keep a card of just first letters.

**Application:** Excellent late-stage tool. Day 4 — when off-script but still leaning on the page — a first-letter card is a graduated cue.

### 1.6 How actors actually memorize (Noice & Noice)
**Evidence: strong, replicated, the closest analog.** Two-decade research program found:
- Actors **do not use rote repetition.** They search for *meaning* and *intention* behind each line.
- "**Active experiencing**" — engaging physically, mentally, and emotionally — outperforms pure memorization. Adult-education AE group recalled 76% vs. 37% for memorization control.
- **Movement helps.** Lines paired with physical action better recalled, even months later.
- **Emotion encodes.** Real emotion "sears in" the words.

**Application:** Daniel's a normal professional, not an actor — but principles port directly. Ask *why* each line is there. Pair emotional beats with intentional physical action (raise glass, step forward). Don't recite — *mean it*.

## 2. Spaced repetition & retrieval practice

### 2.1 Optimal spacing for a 5-day window
**Evidence: very strong (Cepeda, Pashler, Rohrer).** Cepeda 2008 (1,350+ Ss) found **optimal study gap is ~10–20% of retention interval for short delays**, falling toward 5% for very long delays. For 5-day window: gaps of **~12–24 hours** — once or twice per day. Inverted-U: too short = massed (less effective), too long = forgotten.

**Application:** Daily sessions are nearly optimal. Two short sessions per day (morning + evening, ≥6h apart) are even better, particularly with sleep between (see §5).

### 2.2 Testing effect / retrieval practice
**Evidence: very strong (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).** With prose: 3 free-recall tests without feedback massively outperformed 3 restudies, on tests 2 days and 1 week later. At 5 minutes, restudy wins; at any meaningful delay, retrieval crushes restudy.

**Application:** Single biggest lever. After Day 1 (script familiarization), every session should be majority-retrieval. Looking at the script should be the exception, not the rule.

### 2.3 Desirable difficulties
**Evidence: strong (Bjork).** *Successful but effortful* retrieval strengthens memory more than easy retrieval. The catch: difficulty must lead to *successful* retrieval — too hard and you're just failing.

**Application:** Daniel should feel the strain of recall, but should usually succeed. Failing >20–30% of the time = drop back a level.

### 2.4 Interleaving for sequential prose
**Evidence: nuanced.** Interleaving generally beats blocking, but for **sequential material** (where order is the thing being learned), blocked practice yields better immediate sequential recall.

**Application:** Don't randomize chunks. Practice in order. Exception: targeted "weakest section" drill, then re-integrate into a full run.

## 3. Failure modes and recovery

### 3.1 Blanking mid-section: start over, push through, or skip?
**Evidence: largely practitioner; some cognitive grounding.** TOT research shows recovery helped by partial cues (first letter, related word). Acting practice strongly favors **stay in character, paraphrase, push forward** — restarting trains the wrong response.

**Application:** Practice should NOT train restart-on-error. The pre-event behavior: pause briefly (1–2s), paraphrase forward, check the script *only after* the run.

### 3.2 Bridges / transitions are the weak point
**Evidence: well-supported.** In serial recall research, **transitions between rehearsal groups are where errors cluster.** Items within a chunk get rehearsed together; connective tissue between chunks doesn't. This is exactly why cumulative practice helps.

**Application:** For a toast, riskiest moments: opening line, joins between 4–6 chunks, toast line. Over-index on transitions. When re-running after a failure, start *one chunk back*, not from the top.

### 3.3 First-line anchors and serial position effects
**Evidence: very strong (primacy/recency).** First and last items recalled best. Maps to speeches: opening sets state and rhythm, closing lands the emotional payload.

**Application:** First sentence and toast line should be **memorized to over-learning level** — instantly available, emotionally rehearsed, decoupled from the rest. They get extra automatic drills.

## 4. Practice session structure

### 4.1 Massed vs. distributed
**Evidence: very strong.** Distributed doubles or quintuples retention vs. massed at delays of 12–36 hours, even with total time held constant. Overlearning provides no long-term benefit.

**Application:** Four 30-min sessions over four days >> one 2-hour cram. Daniel's 15–30 min pockets are *ideal*. Don't tell him to find a longer block.

### 4.2 How long should a single rep be?
**Evidence: derived.** Ideal rep = whole 2-min speech end-to-end, *plus* targeted micro-drills on weak chunks/bridges. Always ≥1 full pass per session.

### 4.3 Cold start vs. warm-up
**Evidence: moderate (transfer-appropriate processing).** Performance generalizes best when practice conditions match performance. Real toast is a **cold start**. **At least one cold-start rep per session** from Day 3 onward is critical.

### 4.4 Standing, out loud, in front of a mirror
**Evidence: moderate.** Specificity of practice — practicing in conditions matching performance helps. Wedding toast: standing, holding a glass, projecting. Couch + silent trains the wrong state.

## 5. Spoken delivery specifics

### 5.1 Reading aloud vs. silent (production effect)
**Evidence: very strong, replicated.** Reading aloud yields ~10–20% recall advantage. Effect from distinctiveness at encoding.

**Application:** Out loud from session 1. Silent = wrong system.

### 5.2 Recording and listening back
**Evidence: weaker primary; strong practitioner.** Helps with *delivery* (pace, fillers, prosody) more than memorization per se. Useful Day 3–4 to refine *how*.

### 5.3 Walking / movement
**Evidence: mixed-positive.** Movement *paired with content* > non-paired. Pure pacing is mildly helpful; **deliberate gesture tied to meaning** is much more helpful.

### 5.4 Sleep consolidation
**Evidence: strong.** Declarative memory consolidates dramatically better when sleep follows learning within ~3 hours. Material learned 10 hours before bed shows much weaker consolidation than material learned 3 hours before.

**Application:** Daniel's last practice should be **within 2–3 hours of bed** every night. Compounds across 5 nights.

## 6. Five-day timeline: when to lock, when to stop

**Two threshold ideas converge:**
1. **Lock the script early.** Every word change resets the memory trace. Lock by Day 2 at the latest.
2. **Stop hard practice ~12 hours before performance.** Overlearning provides no long-term benefit; counterproductive when crowding sleep/rest. Morning of: one full warm run, then stop.

## 7. Recommended 5-day plan

Assumptions: 2-min toast (~280 words → 4 chunks). Two pockets/day: morning (15–20 min), evening within 2–3 hr of bed (10–15 min). Optional mid-day cold start (5 min).

### Day 1 — Encode for meaning. Don't memorize yet.
**Morning (20 min):** Read aloud slowly 2x, annotate emotional intent per chunk. Mark chunk boundaries. Tag a gesture per chunk. One more performance read-through.
**Evening (15 min, ~2 hr before bed):** Cumulative chunks: chunk 1 from script (3x), 1+2 (2x), 1+2+3 (1x), 1+2+3+4 (1x). Final whole read-through, on feet.
**No memorization pressure today. LOCK THE SCRIPT TONIGHT.**

### Day 2 — First retrievals.
**Morning (20 min):** Anchor drill (opening + toast line) 5x cold. Cumulative from memory (peek allowed). Full speech with script as backup.
**Evening (15 min, near bed):** Full run, script-down, mic on. Note peeks. Drill 1 weak chunk. Final whole run.

### Day 3 — Off-script. Bridges.
**Morning (20 min):** Anchors cold. First-letter view for body, whole speech once. Bridge drill: each chunk-to-chunk transition 3x. Whole run, no script.
**Mid-day (5 min):** Cold start. Open app, deliver immediately. Record.
**Evening (15 min, near bed):** Whole run with intentional gestures. Listen to one playback moment. Final whole run on feet, projecting.

### Day 4 — Performance simulation.
**Morning (20 min):** Anchors cold. Whole run on feet, holding glass. Watch one playback. Bridge drill (only failed bridges). Whole run.
**Mid-day (5 min):** Cold start in unfamiliar location. Record.
**Evening (15 min, near bed):** Whole run, full performance state — projecting, gesturing, eye contact at imagined audience. Final cold-start run.

### Day 5 — Day of toast. Light touch only.
**Morning (15 min):** Anchors cold once. One whole run on feet, full performance. **Stop. Do not over-rehearse.**
**~30 min before:** Run anchors silently in head once. Don't rehearse whole speech immediately before — perform, don't recite.

## 8. Coaching algorithm specification

### Inputs per take
- `daysUntilEvent` (5..0; nullable today)
- `priorTakes` (count)
- `sectionRecallBand[]` per chunk: `green` / `yellow` / `red`
- `bridgeRecallBand[]` per transition (deferred — anchor-level treatment is v2)
- `anchorRecallBand` for opener and closer (deferred — v2)
- `totalRecallScore` (0..1)
- `currentMode` ∈ {script, firstLetter, freeRecall, coldStart}
- `practiceState` ∈ {seated_silent, seated_aloud, standing_aloud, standing_performance}
- `lastSessionAt`, `nextEligibleSessionAt`
- `minutesUntilBed` (deferred)

### Decision tree (priority-ordered)

1. **Day-of (`daysUntilEvent == 0`).** If <2 hr to event: stop, anchors only in head. Else: one full performance run, then stop.
2. **Anchor red.** Drill anchors first, 3x each cold.
3. **Total recall <0.5 AND priorTakes <5.** Encoding phase. Read-through aloud; cumulative chunks. Don't push to free recall yet.
4. **Total recall ≥0.8 AND mode == script.** Promote to firstLetter view.
5. **Total recall ≥0.8 AND mode == firstLetter.** Promote to free recall.
6. **Total recall ≥0.8 AND mode == freeRecall AND daysUntilEvent ≤2.** Promote to cold start.
7. **Any bridge red OR two consecutive yellow.** Bridge drill 3x weak transitions. Then re-run starting one chunk *before* the failed bridge.
8. **Single body chunk red, others green/yellow.** Targeted drill 3x that chunk, then full run. No re-drilling green chunks.
9. **practiceState == seated_silent.** Force-prompt to stand and project.
10. **minutesUntilBed <180 AND no session today.** Surface "night session" prompt.
11. **priorTakes today ≥4 AND recall stable/declining.** Recommend STOP.
12. **daysUntilEvent in {3,2,1} AND no cold-start today.** Schedule cold-start prompt.
13. **After successful full run with recall ≥0.85.** Default = cold-start full run, performance state.
14. **Edits to script after Day 2.** Warn: edits reset memory.

### Mode promotion ladder
`script + audio` → `script silent` → `first-letter view` → `free recall` → `free recall, cold start` → `free recall, cold start, performance state`

Promote up after 2 consecutive sessions ≥80% at current level. Demote one level on a single ≤50% session.

### Spacing scheduler
- Default: 2 sessions/day, ≥6h apart, one ≤3h before bed.
- Optimal gap = 12–24h.
- Skip protection: missed day → next session reverts to "warm-up" mode for first run.

## 9. Anti-patterns to avoid

1. **"Read script aloud 10x in a row."** Trains eyes-on-page state. Switch to retrieval after 1–2 reads.
2. **"Memorize chunk 1 to perfection before chunk 2."** Defers bridge problem. Use cumulative.
3. **"Just read silently."** Loses production effect.
4. **"One long session day before."** Massed practice catastrophically worse than distributed.
5. **"Edit wording mid-week."** Resets memory. Lock by Day 2.
6. **"Restart from top on blank."** Trains restart-on-error. Push forward; resume one chunk back.
7. **"Drill 5 more times to make sure."** Overlearning has no long-term benefit.
8. **"Couch is fine."** Specificity — practice in performance state.
9. **"Use a memory palace."** Overkill for 280 words of meaningful prose.
10. **"Randomize chunk order."** Hurts sequential recall.
11. **"Practice up until the toast starts."** Raises anxiety; replaces performance state with rehearsal state.
12. **"Listen to entire recording every time."** Time-expensive, discouraging. Surface 1–2 specific moments.
13. **"Aim for word-perfect verbatim."** Brittle; not how actors actually perform. Anchor opener + toast line; paraphrase forgiveness for body.
14. **Cold-start metrics on Day 1.** Day 3+ tool. Failing on Day 1 demoralizes.

## 10. Strength-of-evidence summary

| Claim | Strength |
|---|---|
| Distributed > massed practice | Very strong, replicated decades |
| Retrieval > rereading for delayed retention | Very strong (Roediger, Karpicke) |
| Production effect (out loud > silent) | Very strong, replicated |
| Optimal gap ~10–20% of retention interval | Strong (Cepeda meta-analysis) |
| Sleep within ~3h of learning aids consolidation | Strong |
| Method of loci helps ordered lists | Strong (trained); weaker for prose |
| Active experiencing > rote (Noice) | Strong, two-decade program |
| Movement-paired lines recalled better | Moderate-strong |
| Desirable difficulties / effortful retrieval | Strong (Bjork) |
| Interleaving hurts sequential recall | Moderate; consistent with theory |
| Serial position / first-line dominance | Very strong (lab); strong by analogy |
| Overlearning has no long-term benefit | Strong (Rohrer & Pashler) |
| Specificity / state-dependent practice | Moderate-strong |
| Recording-yourself helps memorization | Weak primary; helps delivery |
| Visualization reduces public-speaking anxiety | Moderate; small/indirect on recall |
